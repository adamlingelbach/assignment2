Two Stories, One Dataset

This project looks into complaints against NYPD officers. The purpose of having the two different visualizations is to show a "white hat" visualization which is the first visualization, contrasted with a "black hat" visualization. The "white hat" visualization is to show the data as it is, and effectively so the user can easily interpret what the data is representing. The "black hat" visualization is a manipulation to convey the data in a way that is not accurate.

WHITE HAT: TRANSPARENT

How do allegation outcomes differ by misconduct category?

How do allegation outcomes differ by misconduct category?.

White-hat design write-up

For the white hat visualization, we wanted to clearly show how allegation outcomes differ across four categories of within the NYPD. We used the "Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers" data set. This dataset was compiled by ProPublica and contains civilian complaints filed against NYPD officers. It includes information about the types of alleged misconduct and the outcomes of investigations. For our visualization, we focused specifically on comparing the outcomes of allegations across four misconduct categories. We created a horizontal stacked bar chart that compares Abuse of Authority, Force, Discourtesy, and Offensive Language. The maid difference within this data set is that each category is divided into three possible outcomes: substantiated, unsubstantiated, and exonerated. This was the main design choice that differed with the black-hat because exaunerated really shows a difference, rather than being grouped in with "Unsubstantiated". These outcomes have different meanings, and separating them allows viewers to better understand the results of the allegations.

We chose a horizontal stacked bar chart because it allows viewers to easily compare the proportions of different outcomes across misconduct categories. Rather than displaying the raw number of allegations, we converted the outcomes into percentages so that every bar totals 100%. This makes comparisons easier because some categories may have significantly more allegations than others. We also chose colors that have a high contrast with eachother whic really highlights the difference. The horizontal axis runs from 0% to 100%, ensuring that each category is represented on the same scale. Additionally, we included a dropdown menu that allows users to focus on individual misconduct categories or view all four at once.

Overall, our goal was to make the visualization as clear and accurate as possible. By keeping the three outcomes separate, using consistent percentages, and including clear labels, we allow viewers to interpret the data without being influenced by misleading design choices. We wanted to make sure the visualization accurately represented the differences between misconduct categories while remaining easy for viewers to understand.

BLACK HAT: DELIBERATELY MISLEADING

Most reviewed allegations did not result in substantiation

The misleading techniques we used were 1. data transformation, 2. altering the marks, and 3. adding deceptive titles. See below for more details.

Black-hat Design Write-Up

For our "black hat" visualization, we made several intentional design decisions to influence how the reader interprets the data. Our goal was to make it appear that most complaints against officers were not substantiated, while downplaying the complaints that were substantiated. We used a combination of data transformation, changes to the visual marks, and a misleading title to guide the reader toward this conclusion. Below, we explain each decision and how it contributes to making the visualization misleading.


1. Data Transformation: We decided to aggregate the unsubstantiated and exonerated because the officers were not found guilty of those complaints. There is a subtle difference in the defintions of these terms. Unsubstantiated means there wasn't enough evidence to decide a verdict. Exonerated means that conduct occurred, but it was within acceptable policy. We decided to merge these two categories to be purposefully misleading making it look like the complaints had no evidence behind them, when in fact Exonerated complaints often involved conduct that did occur.
2. Altering the Marks: We changed how the two remaining segments are drawn so the readers eye lands on the outcome we want emphasized. “Not substantiated” is a dark, saturated teal, while “Substantiated” is a pale gray-blue that blends into the white background and reads as leftover space rather than a real outcome. We also placed a bold white percentage label only on the “Not substantiated” segment, so the reader gets an exact number for the dominant bar but has to hover to learn the substantiated rate. Together, these choices make the substantiated share look small and unimportant.
3. Adding Deceptive Titles: The title, “Most reviewed allegations did not result in substantiation,” immediately gives the reader a conclusion before they have a chance to interpret the chart themselves. By using this wording, we guide the reader toward thinking that most complaints against officers were not valid. Combined with the merged categories and altered colors, the title reinforces the message that most complaints did not have enough evidence behind them, even though that is not necessarily what the original data shows.

Data, methods, and limitations

Data Source: ProPublica — Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers (2020) https://www.propublica.org/datastore/dataset/civilian-complaints-against-new-york-city-police-officers

The dataset contains over 12,000 civilian complaints filed against NYPD officers between 1985 and 2020. It includes information about the officers and people filing complaints, such as age, race, gender, rank, and precinct. It also includes the type of complaint and whether the CCRB found the officer violated department rules. However, we decided just to focus on the subset of the complaint type, and outcome of complaint. ProPublica collected the data through a public records requests. One limitation is that the data relies on the NYPD to provide evidence, such as body camera footage, which can make it difficult to reach a conclusion. Another limitation is that the dataset only includes officers who were still in NYPD in June 2020 and had at least one substantiated complaint.

AI usage disclosure

Throughout our development of this assignment, we used AI to help us in 2 ways. The first way we used AI to help us was for brainstorming how we could make the visualization purposefully misleading. The first thing as a group we needed to understand was the meaning of the vocabulary within our dataset. AI was helpful in explaining terms we had never heard of through using simple analogies. Once we understood what Unsubstantiated, Substantiated, and Exonerated meant in the context of the NYPD officers, we decided to aggregate the data into two categories instead of the original three for the "black hat" visualization. We wanted to be purposefully misleading with our data aggregation, so we split the data into "Substantiated" or "Not substantiated" to describe the outcome of the civilian complaints. This in turn helped the data look misleading because many of the complaints did not have substance to back them up. Furthermore, we used AI in helping us generate the marks to make it so that the Non-Substantiated bars appeared much darker and more eye catching then the substantiated ones.

The second way we used AI was aiding in the development of creating our 100% stacked bar chart and the styling of our webpage. When we first started development, we decided as a group to use a 100% stacked bar chart. We knew that this would be the simplest for our dataset because there were 3 different categories, within 4 different types of complaints. We prompted AI to give us a good color scheme for our stacked bar chart. We also used AI for the styling of our webpage, to seperate into clean sections and boxes to make our webpage more user friendly. For example, we prompted to seperate our page into clean rounded boxes for the "white hat" and "black hat" visualizations. Lastly, we used AI to help us come up with the "Reveal The Manipulation / Raw Data" button to demonstrate how the user can directly see how we manipulated the data. This was a good feature to add because the comparison from the "white hat" to the "black hat" can be done with just a click.

Extra credit feature: Reveal the manipulation

For our extra credit feature, we decided to add an interactive button to the black hat visualization that allows users to switch to the white hat visualization. We wanted to include this feature because it gives viewers the opportunity to directly compare how the same dataset can be presented in two different ways. By switching between the visualizations, users can see how combining unsubstantiated and exonerated outcomes, changing colors, and emphasizing certain information can influence how the data is interpreted. This feature goes beyond simply explaining the misleading techniques because it allows users to visually recognize the differences themselves. Our goal was to make the website more interactive while also showing how important transparency and accurate design choices are when presenting data.
